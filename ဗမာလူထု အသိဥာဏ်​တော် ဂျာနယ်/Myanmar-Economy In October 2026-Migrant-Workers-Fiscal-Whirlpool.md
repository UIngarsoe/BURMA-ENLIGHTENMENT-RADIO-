## Myanmar’s Economy and the Financial Whirlpool of Migrant Workers
A Mathematical and Empirical Analysis of Cost-Push Inflation, Stagflation, and Sovereign Fiscal Stress

An Independent Academic Assessment Grounded in Multidimensional Systems Analysis (SSISM / MYISM Framework)
Working Paper | 12 October 2026

#### Author: U Ingar Soe Burma Enlightenment Journal 84


## Abstract

This paper examines the concurrent economic and fiscal pressures confronting Myanmar in 2025–2026, with particular attention to the financial burdens imposed on overseas migrant workers. Drawing on data from the World Bank, Asian Development Bank (ADB), and contemporaneous media reporting, we demonstrate that Myanmar has entered a regime of cost-push inflation combined with stagnant real activity—i.e., stagflation—while fiscal deficits and monetary financing raise the risk of sovereign fiscal stress. Mandatory remittance rules (25 percent of wages through official channels) and a 2 percent income tax on overseas earnings are analyzed as instruments of foreign-exchange extraction. Mathematical models of aggregate supply shocks, margin squeeze, demand destruction, and debt dynamics are presented. The analysis concludes that without timely reform of the tax-and-remittance regime, exchange-rate policy, and political stabilization, the economic and social whirlpool is likely to deepen.

## 1. Introduction and Analytical Framework

Myanmar’s economy continues to operate under severe constraints five years after the 2021 military takeover. Real GDP contracted further in FY2025 (ending March 2026), inflation remains in the high teens to mid-twenties, and household purchasing power has been eroded by cumulative price increases that have more than tripled since the coup. Simultaneously, an estimated several million Myanmar nationals work abroad—principally in Thailand—and constitute a critical source of foreign-exchange inflows.
The State Administration Council (SAC) has responded by imposing mandatory family remittances of at least 25 percent of wages through official banking channels and a 2 percent income tax on overseas earnings. These measures, while generating hard-currency receipts, impose substantial effective losses on workers because of the gap between official and parallel-market exchange rates.
This paper adopts a multidimensional systems-analytic perspective (SSISM / MYISM) that integrates macroeconomic aggregates, firm-level margin dynamics, household-level welfare effects, and institutional extraction mechanisms.

## 2. Ground Realities: Cost-Push Inflation, Margin Squeeze, and Stagflation

2.1 Cost-Push Inflation and the Aggregate Supply Shock
Cost-push inflation arises when exogenous increases in input prices (fuel, logistics, imported intermediates, energy) shift the short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) schedule leftward. In a simple linear specification:
$$ P = P_0 + \alpha(Y - Y^*) + \beta Z $$
where:
( P ) = price level
( Y ) = real output
( Y^* ) = potential output
( Z ) = vector of cost-push factors (fuel prices, exchange-rate depreciation, supply-chain disruption)
( \alpha, \beta > 0 )
An increase in ( Z ) raises ( P ) for any given ( Y ), producing simultaneous inflation and output contraction—the hallmark of stagflation.
Empirical evidence confirms this mechanism. World Bank and ADB data show headline inflation remaining elevated (approximately 18–25 percent year-on-year through late 2025 and early 2026) despite intermittent moderation, driven by fuel shocks, logistics costs, and import restrictions. Real GDP contracted by an estimated 2.0–2.2 percent in FY2025/26. Services and industry both declined, consistent with a leftward SRAS shift rather than pure demand-pull inflation.

2.2 Margin Squeeze and Demand Destruction
At the firm level, cost-push shocks compress operating margins. Let a representative firm’s profit margin be:
$$ \mu = \frac{P - c}{P} = 1 - \frac{c}{P} $$
where ( c ) is unit cost. When cost shocks raise ( c ) faster than firms can pass them through to ( P ) (owing to weak demand or price controls), ( \mu ) falls. Small restaurants and micro-enterprises, which operate with thin buffers, experience the sharpest squeeze.
The resulting reduction in real incomes and employment further erodes aggregate demand—a process of demand destruction. Formally:
$$ \Delta Y^{d} = -\eta \cdot \Delta\left(\frac{P}{W}\right) $$
where ( \eta ) is the elasticity of demand with respect to the real price of consumption (or the inverse of real wages). When real wages decline continuously under high inflation, household consumption contracts, reinforcing the output gap.
World Bank firm surveys indicate that only a minority of enterprises reported rising sales or profits in 2025–2026; the majority faced weak demand, higher input costs, and power outages. This constellation is the micro-foundation of the macro stagflation observed in national accounts.

2.3 Stagflation as the Dominant Regime
Stagflation is defined as the simultaneous occurrence of:
High inflation
Stagnant or negative growth
Elevated under-employment
Myanmar satisfies all three conditions:
Inflation persistently above 18 percent
Successive years of GDP contraction or near-zero growth since the coup
Labor-market distress manifested in large-scale out-migration and skill shortages in remaining domestic sectors
The policy dilemma is classic: demand stimulus risks accelerating inflation, while demand restraint deepens the output gap. Supply-side remedies (restoring logistics, energy, and security) are constrained by ongoing conflict and institutional capacity.

## 3. Sovereign Fiscal Pressure and the Path toward Fiscal Stress
Fiscal accounts reveal widening deficits. World Bank estimates place the fiscal deficit at approximately 4.9–5.5 percent of GDP in FY2025/26, driven by reconstruction needs after the March 2025 earthquake, election-related outlays, and weak tax revenue. Public debt remains elevated (around 62–64 percent of GDP). Critically, a substantial portion of deficit financing has been met by central-bank credit or commercial-bank absorption of government paper, expanding the monetary base.
The basic debt-dynamics identity is:
$$ \Delta d = (r - g)d + pd - \text{seigniorage} $$
where:
( d ) = debt-to-GDP ratio
( r ) = real interest rate
( g ) = real growth
( pd ) = primary deficit
When growth is negative or near zero and the primary balance is in deficit, debt rises unless offset by inflationary seigniorage or financial repression. Persistent monetary financing of deficits feeds the cost-push channel via exchange-rate pressure and expectations, closing a vicious circle between fiscal imbalance and inflation.
ADB and World Bank projections show the fiscal deficit remaining elevated (around 4.5–5.2 percent of GDP) through FY2026/27. Without credible consolidation and a restoration of non-inflationary financing, the trajectory is consistent with rising sovereign stress—though not yet an outright default, given limited external market access and reliance on domestic absorption.

## 4. Financial Burdens on Migrant Workers

4.1 Mandatory Remittance Rule
Since September 2023, and with intensified enforcement from August 2024, overseas Myanmar workers—especially those under the Thailand MoU “U-turn” process—must demonstrate that they have remitted at least 25 percent of their wages (or a minimum of 6,000 Thai baht over a three-month reference period) through official banking channels or licensed remittance providers.
Failure to produce the remittance slip can result in non-renewal of work permits, Overseas Worker Identification Cards (OWIC), or passports.
The economic incidence of the rule is severe because the official exchange rate offered by regime-linked banks has historically been far below the parallel-market rate. If the official rate is approximately half the market rate, a worker forced to remit 6,000 baht officially forgoes roughly half the domestic purchasing power that the same sum would have commanded via informal (hundi) channels.
Formally, the effective loss is:
$$ \text{Loss} = R \cdot \frac{e_m - e_o}{e_m} $$
where:
( R ) = mandated remittance amount
( e_m ) = market rate (kyat per baht)
( e_o ) = official rate
When ( e_o \approx 0.5 , e_m ), the loss approaches 50 percent of the remitted sum—an implicit tax of large magnitude.

4.2 Income Tax on Overseas Earnings
In parallel, a 2 percent income tax on foreign earnings was introduced under the amended Union Taxation Law effective October 2023.
For blue-collar workers the reference monthly income is set at 7,500 baht → monthly liability of 150 baht (or 1,800 baht on an annual basis).
White-collar workers face higher absolute liabilities scaled to reported salaries.
Proof of tax payment is required for CI (Certificate of Identity) renewal, passport services, and related documentation. Some media reports and worker accounts have described the cumulative payments as effectively treated by authorities as “contributions” or “donations,” reflecting the extractive framing of the policy.

4.3 Aggregate Foreign-Exchange Extraction
Despite the individual burden, the policy has succeeded in elevating recorded remittance inflows. Independent estimates indicate official remittances rose from roughly USD 0.67 billion in 2022 to approximately USD 5.6 billion in 2025, becoming the largest single source of foreign-currency inflow.
This surge provides a temporary fiscal and external buffer, yet it rests on coercive compliance and an exchange-rate wedge that transfers surplus from households to the state and its banking intermediaries.

## 5. Conclusion and Policy Implications

The economic and fiscal pressures documented above are not abstract theoretical constructs; they translate directly into reduced real incomes, business closures, and heightened livelihood insecurity for both domestic households and the large diaspora of migrant workers.
Cost-push inflation interacting with weak demand has produced a stagflationary equilibrium.
Fiscal deficits financed in part by monetary expansion reinforce inflationary expectations.
Mandatory remittances and overseas income taxation extract foreign exchange at the cost of substantial welfare losses for workers and their families.
Recommended Corrective Measures
Narrowing the official–parallel exchange-rate gap to reduce the effective tax on remittances.
Rationalizing the overseas income-tax regime so that it does not function as a pure extraction device.
Restoring non-inflationary sources of fiscal finance and strengthening tax administration on a broader base.
Addressing the security and logistical constraints that underlie the cost-push shocks.
Absent such reforms, the interaction of stagflation, fiscal stress, and extractive labor policies is likely to deepen the economic and social whirlpool facing Myanmar.
